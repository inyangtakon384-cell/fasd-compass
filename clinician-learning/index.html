FASD Learning Modules for Clinicians

Six short modules for clinical teams, with key points and questions to reflect on.
FASD Compass · fasdcompass.co.uk/clinician-learning/
Six short modules for clinicians, GPs, nurses and allied health professionals. Each takes about five to ten minutes and has key points, a question to reflect on and links to the source guidance. Use them for self-directed learning or team teaching.
🩺
An educational resource, not a diagnostic tool
These resources support clinical reasoning. They do not score, diagnose or rule out FASD, and they do not decide who should be referred. Diagnosis needs a multidisciplinary assessment using the SIGN 156 criteria.

These modules are not accredited for CPD. You can record your own reflections in your CPD portfolio if your professional body allows it.

1 Recognising possible FASD

Key points

  • FASD is common but often missed. Most people with FASD do not have the facial features.
  • Consider prenatal alcohol exposure when a child has unexplained neurodevelopmental difficulties, or a profile that is uneven or not responding to usual support.
  • Typical patterns include difficulties with memory, executive function, language understanding, attention, regulation and adaptive skills, often with good expressive language that hides the difficulties.
  • None of these patterns are specific to FASD. They also occur in ADHD, autism, trauma, language disorder and learning difficulties.
  • Children who are looked after or adopted have higher rates of prenatal alcohol exposure. Being in care is not evidence of exposure.

Reflect

A 9-year-old has an ADHD diagnosis and has not responded as expected to medication. Their carers describe forgetting skills from one day to the next. What else would you ask about?

2 Sensitive history taking

Key points

  • Ask every family about alcohol in pregnancy, in the same way you ask about other exposures. This makes it routine rather than accusing.
  • Use non-blaming language, such as “when alcohol was consumed during pregnancy”.
  • Record the type, amount, pattern, frequency and timing of drinking, and who gave the information.
  • Record exposure as confirmed, confirmed absent or unknown. Unknown is not the same as absent.
  • Some birth parents may themselves have been affected by prenatal alcohol exposure. Adapt how you share information.

3 Diagnostic frameworks

Key points

  • In the UK, SIGN 156 (2019) is the diagnostic guideline. NICE QS204 (2022) sets quality standards for England.
  • SIGN 156 uses two diagnostic terms: FASD with sentinel facial features and FASD without sentinel facial features. It also has a designation for people at risk of FASD.
  • Pervasive brain dysfunction means severe impairment in three or more of the 10 areas of assessment.
  • Older terms such as FAS, pFAS, ARND and ARBD are still used in other countries and in older reports.
  • Genetic factors should be considered in all cases.

4 FASD and co-occurring neurodevelopmental conditions

Key points

  • Many children with FASD do also have ADHD. FASD and ADHD can both be diagnosed.
  • Under SIGN 156, a diagnosis of ADHD (inattentive or combined presentation) meets the criteria for severe impairment in attention.
  • Autism, intellectual disability, language disorder, developmental coordination disorder, sleep problems, anxiety and depression are also common.
  • Trauma and changes of carer can affect regulation and attachment. SIGN 156 advises not to attribute affect regulation difficulties to alcohol exposure when they are better explained by life events.
  • A good formulation names all the contributors. It does not force everything into one diagnosis.

5 Communication with families

Key points

  • Explain what the assessment involves, including photographs, and gain informed consent.
  • Share results with the family and, where appropriate, the child or young person, in clear language.
  • Give a written or visual summary they can take away and share.
  • Parents and carers may feel grief, loss, anger or guilt. Say that these feelings are normal, and offer support.
  • Focus on strengths and practical next steps, not only on the diagnosis.

6 Coordinating support

Key points

  • Every child with a diagnosis should have a management plan to address their needs (NICE QS204 Statement 5).
  • Base the plan on the child’s strengths and difficulties, not on the label alone.
  • Share the plan with school, with consent, so it can be built into SEN or ALN support.
  • A member of the team should follow up within a specified time to check that recommendations have happened.
  • Plan reassessment at transitions, such as starting secondary school and moving to adult services.
